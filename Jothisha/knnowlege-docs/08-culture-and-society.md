# Astrology in Sri Lankan Culture and Society

Astrology — **Jothisha** or **Hela Jothisha** — is not confined to horoscope charts and planetary tables in Sri Lanka; it is woven into the rhythm of everyday and public life. From the moment a national New Year begins to the timing of a funeral, from a newspaper's back page to a government gazette, astrologically determined "auspicious time" (**nekatha** or **nekath**, නැකත) shapes how millions of Sri Lankans — mostly Sinhala Buddhists, but also Hindus and, informally, some members of other communities — schedule the events that matter to them. This document surveys that social role: the Sinhala and Tamil New Year, life-cycle rituals, the uneasy but enduring relationship between astrology and Buddhism, astrology's presence in the media, and the state's own role in fixing the nation's official auspicious times.

## The Sinhala and Tamil New Year (Aluth Avurudda) and Nekath

The clearest, most widely observed expression of applied astrology in Sri Lanka is the Sinhala and Tamil New Year, **Aluth Avurudda**, celebrated in mid-April (usually 13–14 April) to mark the sun's transit from the sign Meena (Pisces) into Mesha (Aries). Unlike a fixed civil New Year, Aluth Avurudda is structured almost entirely around a sequence of astrologically calculated moments — the **nekath** — that dictate when each ritual act should be performed. A period called the **nonagathe** or **Punya Kalaya** ("neutral time" or "period of merit") falls between the old and new astrological years; during this interval no worldly or income-generating activity is meant to be undertaken, and households instead devote the time to religious observance, rest, and preparation.

Once the new year formally dawns at its calculated moment, a chain of nekath governs, in order: lighting the hearth and boiling milk to symbolize prosperity, cooking and eating the first meal of the year (typically kiribath, milk rice), conducting the first financial transaction of the year (**ganu-denu**, exchanging money or gifts, often between family members), anointing the head with medicinal oil (**hisa thel gaama** — sometimes performed at a temple by a monk), and setting off to start work for the year. Each moment carries a specific clock time, a direction to face, and sometimes a colour of clothing to wear, calculated in advance from planetary positions. Following the nekath precisely is widely believed to shape the household's health, harmony, and prosperity for the coming year, and the rituals are performed by the whole family together, reinforcing kinship bonds alongside their astrological purpose.

## Nekatha for Other Life-Cycle Events

Beyond the New Year, consulting an astrologer for an auspicious time is a routine part of major Sri Lankan life events, cutting across income levels and, informally, across some religious lines:

- **Weddings**: An astrologer is consulted well before the wedding date, matching the horoscopes (**porondam**) of the bride and groom for compatibility and then fixing not just the day but the precise moment for the couple to step onto the **poruwa** (the ceremonial platform), exchange rings, and tie the ceremonial thread — all timed to the minute.
- **Moving into a new house**: A "housewarming" or first-entry nekatha is fixed for the family's first entry into a new or newly built home, often paired with a ritual boiling of milk on the new hearth.
- **Starting a business**: Opening a new shop, signing a business agreement, or beginning a new venture is frequently scheduled for an astrologically approved moment, believed to set the tone for the enterprise's fortunes.
- **First rice-feeding (Indul Kiribath / Nam Bath)**: A baby's first solid meal, usually rice, is fed at an auspicious moment calculated from the child's own birth chart, usually around six months of age.
- **Ear-piercing ceremony**: Piercing a baby girl's ears is likewise commonly timed to a nekatha drawn from her horoscope.
- **First day of school / first letter-writing**: The day a child is first taken to school, or made to trace their first letters (an initiation into literacy), is often fixed astrologically.
- **Funerals**: Even in bereavement, astrology plays a role — the timing of the funeral procession and cremation or burial can be chosen with reference to auspicious or inauspicious periods, alongside the Buddhist monastic rites that form the core of the ceremony.

In each case, the underlying logic is the same one used in horoscope work generally: a birth chart is drawn up near birth and consulted throughout life, and a family astrologer (often a trusted individual consulted across generations) is asked to identify a moment when planetary influences are believed to favour the undertaking.

## Astrology and Buddhism: Doctrine versus Practice

Sri Lanka's relationship between astrology and its majority religion, Theravada Buddhism, is genuinely paradoxical, and it is worth describing that tension plainly rather than resolving it artificially.

On the doctrinal side, early Buddhist texts are explicit. The Brahmajala Sutta and Samannaphala Sutta list astrology (along with palmistry, interpreting omens, predicting eclipses and celestial events, and forecasting the fortunes of rulers and armies) among the **"tiracchana-vijja"** — literally "animal knowledge," or low arts — that a monk keeping to "moral practice" abstains from. Earning a living by such practices is classed in the texts as a form of **"wrong livelihood" (miccha-ajiva)**, unbecoming of a renunciant focused on liberation, and the Buddha is recorded as discouraging attachment to such predictions as a distraction from the path.

In practice, however, astrology has long been entangled with Sinhala Buddhist life, temples included. Kings historically retained court astrologers to time coronations and the construction of religious monuments; many temples still offer the New Year head-anointing ritual; and it is not unusual to find individual monks who are also practising astrologers, or lay astrologers whose consultations sit comfortably alongside a family's temple observances. Sinhala Buddhist families routinely treat consulting an astrologer and consulting a monk as complementary rather than contradictory acts when planning a wedding, a house move, or a funeral. The result is a widely observed coexistence rather than doctrinal reconciliation: formal monastic teaching still categorizes astrology as a peripheral or improper pursuit for a monk, while at the level of lived culture — including in the New Year rites the state itself organizes — astrology functions as an accepted, largely unquestioned part of Sinhala Buddhist identity. Sri Lankan commentators have periodically written about this tension, questioning astrology's social and economic influence even while acknowledging how thoroughly it is embedded in cultural and religious routine.

## Astrology in the Media

Astrology has a substantial, continuous presence in Sri Lankan mass media. Sinhala- and Tamil-language newspapers routinely carry horoscope columns predicting the week or month ahead by zodiac sign or nakshatra, written by named astrologers who build public followings through their columns. Television and radio stations feature dedicated astrology segments and call-in programs, particularly around the New Year season, when the release of the year's nekath and forecasts for each zodiac sign become prominent news in their own right. Some astrologers have achieved something close to celebrity status through decades of media appearances and predictions concerning politics, the economy, and national events, alongside their private consultation practice. The internet era has added a large number of Sri Lankan astrology websites and apps offering horoscope generation, matching services, and New Year nekath tables translated into multiple time zones for the diaspora. Underlying this media activity is the same institution found in private life: many families maintain a long-standing relationship with a particular family astrologer, consulted directly and privately for major decisions — marriage proposals, house purchases, career choices, health concerns — rather than relying solely on generic published horoscopes.

## The State's Role: Official Nekath for the Nation

Uniquely among the events described above, the Sinhala and Tamil New Year auspicious times are not left to individual or family astrologers alone — they are calculated and issued nationally so the whole country observes the same schedule. A body generally referred to as the **State Astrologers' Committee**, operating under the **Department of Cultural Affairs** (in some years reported under the Ministry of Buddhasasana, Religious and Cultural Affairs), prepares the definitive **"Nekath Seettuwa"** (auspicious-times table) months in advance. This table is formally presented to the President and then published for the public, specifying the New Year's dawning moment, the Punya Kalaya, and the times for hearth-lighting, the first meal, transactions, anointing, and departure for work — calculated to standard clock time so they can be observed uniformly across the island (and adapted to local time zones by the diaspora abroad). This annual publication is arguably the most visible institutional endorsement of astrology in Sri Lankan public life: a modern nation-state formally producing and disseminating an astrological ritual calendar as an act of cultural administration, alongside its otherwise secular governance and official recognition of Buddhism.
