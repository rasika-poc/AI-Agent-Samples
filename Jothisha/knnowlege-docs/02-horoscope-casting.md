# Casting a Horoscope (Kendaraya) in Sri Lankan Astrology

In Sri Lankan Sinhala astrology — Hela Jothisha — a birth chart is called a **Kendaraya** (කේන්දරය), sometimes written "Kendare" in colloquial usage. It is regarded as a fixed record of the sky at the exact moment a person was born, and it forms the basis for lifelong predictions, compatibility matching (Porondam), and the timing of auspicious moments (Nekatha/Nekath). Casting a Kendaraya follows the same Vedic (Jyotisha) computational framework used across South Asia, adapted to local convention in chart layout and everyday practice. This article outlines how a horoscope is drawn up: the data it depends on, the visual convention used to lay it out, the five-fold calendrical calculation (Panchanga) behind it, how planets are placed, and how the finished chart is used to find auspicious timing.

## Birth Data: Date, Time, and Place

Three inputs are indispensable for casting a Kendaraya: the exact date of birth, the exact time of birth, and the precise place of birth (used to obtain latitude, longitude, and time-zone offset).

Precision of birth time matters because the chart pivots on the **Lagna** (ascendant) — the zodiacal sign rising on the eastern horizon at the moment of birth. The ascendant shifts roughly one sign every two hours, and even a few minutes' error can shift the degree within a sign enough to change which house a planet falls into, or which of the 27 lunar mansions (Nakshatra) the Moon occupies. Because the house cusps, the Moon's Nakshatra (which anchors the Vimshottari Dasha planetary-period timeline), and the divisional charts all depend on this one moment, astrologers stress recording the birth minute as accurately as possible — historically by noting the time a birth cry was heard, today from hospital records. Place of birth is equally necessary because it fixes the local horizon used to compute the ascendant and the correction from local clock time to astronomical time.

## The Chart-Drawing Convention

Sri Lankan horoscopes, like those of South India, are traditionally drawn as a **square divided into a fixed grid of twelve smaller boxes**, arranged in a 4x4 outer square with the four central cells left open as a diamond in the middle (the classic South Indian, or "Dravida," format). Here the twelve zodiac signs (Rashis) occupy fixed positions in the grid — Aries always in the same box, Taurus in the next, and so on clockwise. What changes from person to person is which sign is marked as the first house (Lagna); the astrologer marks whichever box holds the ascending sign, and the houses are read off in order around the fixed signs.

This differs from the **North Indian style**, a diamond-shaped chart where the *houses* are fixed (the top-center wedge is always the first house) and the *signs* rotate into place depending on the individual's ascendant. Both conventions encode identical astronomical information; they differ only in what is held fixed on the page. Sri Lankan practice has historically followed the South Indian square-box layout, including in the older practice of inscribing horoscopes onto treated talipot palm leaves (Talpatha) with a metal stylus, ruling out the grid with measuring tools before entering the planetary positions.

## The Panchanga: Five Calendrical Elements

Before planets are placed, a Sri Lankan astrologer calculates the **Panchanga** ("five limbs") for the birth moment — the same five-fold almanac system used to construct the traditional Sinhala calendar and determine festival and ritual timings generally:

- **Tithi (lunar day):** the angular distance between the Sun and Moon, divided into 30 tithis per lunar month (15 waxing, Sukla Paksha, and 15 waning, Krishna Paksha), each spanning roughly 12 degrees of Moon-minus-Sun separation.
- **Vara (weekday):** the day of the week, each ruled by one of the seven classical planets, giving the day a particular planetary character.
- **Nakshatra (lunar mansion/birth star):** the segment of sky (one of 27 divisions of 13°20' each) occupied by the Moon at birth — often called the person's "birth star," central to personality assessment, compatibility matching, and the Vimshottari Dasha (planetary period) timeline.
- **Yoga:** the sum of the Sun's and Moon's longitudes divided into 27 segments of 13°20' each, yielding one of 27 named Yogas that colors the general quality of the time.
- **Karana:** half of a tithi (roughly 6 degrees of separation), a finer subdivision used to pinpoint especially favorable or unfavorable short intervals within a day.

These five elements together describe the "quality" of a moment in time and are the same building blocks used later to select Nekatha (auspicious times) for future events.

## Placing the Navagraha: Rashi and Bhava

With the Panchanga established, the astrologer computes the positions of the **Navagraha** — the nine "planets" of Vedic astrology: the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, and the two lunar nodes Rahu and Ketu. This traditionally involves converting the recorded clock time into sidereal (star-based) time adjusted for local mean time, applying the **ayanamsa** correction (see below) to obtain each planet's true sidereal longitude, then determining which of the twelve **Rashis** (zodiac signs) each planet occupies and which **Bhava** (house, one of twelve life-areas counted from the ascendant) it falls into. House placement matters as much as sign, since it governs which department of life a planet most strongly influences — for example the seventh house (Kalatra Bhava) relates to marriage, the tenth to career, the twelfth to loss and spiritual matters.

Each planet's Rashi and Bhava are marked into the corresponding fixed box of the square chart. Traditionally an astrologer would also assess each planet's relative strength (Shadbala) and dignity (exaltation, debilitation, own sign) to weigh how strongly its influence should be read. Modern practitioners in Sri Lanka generally use astrological software or ephemeris tables for these calculations, though the chart is still drawn and read in the traditional square format, and a hand-inscribed keepsake is sometimes still produced for ceremonial value.

## Sidereal vs. Tropical Zodiac

Sri Lankan Jothisha, like Vedic astrology across South Asia, calculates planetary positions using the **sidereal zodiac**, fixed relative to the background stars, rather than the **tropical zodiac** used in Western astrology, fixed relative to the Sun's position at the March equinox. Because of the slow precession of the Earth's axis, the two zodiacs have drifted apart by roughly 24 degrees, a gap called the **ayanamsa** that widens by about one degree every 72 years and must be applied to convert a planet's tropical longitude into the sidereal longitude used in a Kendaraya. Sidereal reckoning matters here because the 27 Nakshatras are defined against fixed star groups; if the zodiac drifted with the equinox as in tropical astrology, the Nakshatra, birth-star, and Dasha calculations built on it would no longer correspond to the actual sky.

## Nekatha: Auspicious Time Derived from the Horoscope

Once a person's Kendaraya is cast, it becomes the reference chart against which future auspicious moments — **Nekatha** or **Nekath** (නැකත්) — are calculated. Rather than simply avoiding unlucky moments, Nekatha aims to begin an undertaking at a time whose Panchanga qualities (Tithi, Vara, Nakshatra, Yoga, Karana) and current planetary transits harmonize with the person's own birth chart, giving the endeavor the best chance of a favorable outcome.

Nekatha calculation is a routine, highly visible part of everyday life in Sri Lanka. Common occasions include: starting a journey or new venture; ear-piercing ceremonies for infant girls; the first rice-feeding ceremony for infants; weddings (the ceremony, homecoming, and first shared meal); entering a newly built or purchased home and lighting the hearth for the first time; beginning a child's education; opening a business or making a first sale; and the Sinhala and Tamil New Year, whose sequence of nationally observed auspicious moments — lighting the hearth, preparing meals, starting work, anointing with oil, commencing travel — is calculated annually and published for the whole country to follow.

Because Nekatha depends on the individual's own Kendaraya as well as the general Panchanga of the day, the same calendar date can yield different recommended times for different people — which is why families often consult a personal astrologer for major life events even when a general almanac's public auspicious-times list already exists.
