# Sri Lankan Astrology (Jothisha / Hela Jothisha): Overview and History

Sri Lankan astrology — known in Sinhala as **Jyothishaya** (ජ්‍යෝතිෂය, "the science of light") and sometimes referred to as **Hela Jothisha** ("Sinhala/indigenous astrology") — is the island's centuries-old tradition of reading planetary positions, birth charts, and auspicious timing to guide personal, religious, and civic life. It is practiced overwhelmingly among the Sinhalese Buddhist population, though comparable practices exist among Sri Lankan Tamils and Hindus. This document gives a factual overview of what the tradition is, where it came from, and how it continues to function in Sri Lankan society today.

## Relationship to Indian Vedic Jyotish

Sri Lankan Jyothishaya belongs to the same broad family as Indian **Jyotisha** (Vedic astrology) and shares its foundational technical apparatus:

- **Nakshatras** — the 27 lunar mansions used to mark the Moon's position and name auspicious/inauspicious moments.
- **Rashis** — the 12 zodiacal signs.
- **Grahas** — the nine astrological "planets" (Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, and the lunar nodes Rahu and Ketu).
- **Dasha** systems — the planetary period sequences used to time life events.

These elements arrived in Sri Lanka via long-standing contact with South India and were adapted using the same sidereal (nirayana) zodiac as Indian Jyotisha, rather than the tropical zodiac used in Western astrology.

Despite this shared toolkit, Sri Lankan practice differs from mainstream Indian Vedic astrology in a few respects:

- **Chart-drawing conventions.** South Asian horoscope charts are typically drawn in "North Indian," "South Indian," or "Bengali/Kerala" styles. Sri Lankan astrology has its own recognized chart format, generally grouped with the South Indian square-diagram family but treated as a distinct "Sri Lankan" style in astrological software and manuals. Practitioners commonly work with two primary birth charts — the **Lagna** (ascendant) chart and the **Nawamsa/Nawansaka** chart — alongside several supplementary divisional charts.
- **Regional vocabulary and texts.** Sinhala astrology uses its own terminology (e.g., "nekath" for auspicious time, "porondam" for marriage-compatibility matching) and locally compiled almanacs, distinct from the Sanskrit classical corpus (*Brihat Jataka*, *Saravali*, *Jataka Parijata*, etc.) that anchors Indian Jyotisha.
- **Cultural and religious integration.** Indian Jyotisha is embedded in a Hindu theological framework in which planets (grahas) are often treated as deities to be propitiated through ritual. Sri Lankan astrology instead developed within a Theravada Buddhist setting and tends to reinterpret planetary influence through the lens of **karma and personal conduct**: a difficult planetary period is read as a karmic tendency to be met with ethical conduct, merit-making, and meditation, alongside (not instead of) conventional remedies. This is often described as Vedic technique read through a Buddhist philosophical lens — shared calculation, distinct interpretive spirit.

## Historical Origins and Development

Astrological and calendrical knowledge in Sri Lanka is generally traced to sustained cultural contact with India from antiquity, arriving alongside Buddhism, Brahmi script, and other elements of Indian civilization from around the 3rd century BCE. Early astronomical/astrological practice predates the formal establishment of Buddhism as state religion and was initially associated with Brahmin ritual specialists before being absorbed into Sinhala Buddhist court and monastic life.

The island's great Pali chronicles, the **Mahavamsa** ("Great Chronicle," compiled c. 5th century CE) and its continuation the **Culavamsa** ("Lesser Chronicle," covering the 4th century to 1815), record astrology as an established part of statecraft from the earliest periods of Sinhala kingship. The Mahavamsa recounts that **Anuradhapura**, the ancient capital, was founded under the auspicious lunar asterism "Anura" (the Anuradha nakshatra), tying the city's founding to astrological timing. The chronicles also describe kings consulting astrologers before military campaigns, coronations, and other major undertakings, and record astrologically significant royal births and reigns. King **Dutugemunu** (r. 161–137 BCE) is further credited with assigning a physician, an astrologer, and a priest to serve every group of sixteen villages — astrological service treated, at least in later retellings, as a public good alongside medicine and religion.

Through the medieval period, astrology remained closely tied to **Buddhist monasteries**, which served as centers of learning where monks taught astrology to lay and monastic students within teacher-lineages known as **Jyotisha Gurukula**. Knowledge was transmitted through palm-leaf manuscripts (*ola* books) and oral instruction, and royal courts retained astrologers as formal advisors on the timing of coronations, warfare, marriages, and temple construction. Popular tradition also attributes early astrological knowledge to the legendary king **Ravana**, associated with a work called the *Ravana Samhita*, though this belongs to folk and devotional tradition rather than a documented text lineage.

Because Sri Lankan astrology developed through localized monastic and family transmission rather than a single canonical textual tradition, it lacks one dominant named treatise equivalent to the Sanskrit classics that anchor Indian Jyotisha (e.g., *Brihat Jataka* of Varahamihira, *Saravali* of Kalyana Varma). Instead, practitioners typically draw on a mix of inherited Sanskrit/Indian classics, locally compiled Sinhala almanacs (litha), and knowledge passed down through gurukula-style lineages.

## The Practice Today

Astrology remains a visible and functioning institution in modern Sri Lankan life:

- **Professional astrologers ("nekath nakath" specialists).** Practicing astrologers — often consulted for "nekath" (auspicious time) determinations — are called upon for weddings, house-warmings, business openings, travel, naming ceremonies, and marriage-compatibility ("porondam") matching. Consulting an astrologer before a major life decision is routine, mainstream practice for many Sinhalese Buddhist (and Tamil Hindu) families rather than a fringe activity.
- **Newspaper astrology columns.** Sinhala-language newspapers (such as *Dinamina*, *Divaina*, and *Lankadeepa*) and their websites carry regular astrology content, including predictions and notices of auspicious times, reflecting continued public demand for astrological guidance.
- **State and public functions.** The most visible official role of astrology is around the **Sinhala and Tamil New Year (Aluth Avurudda)**. Sri Lanka's Department of Cultural Affairs maintains a government-appointed committee of astrologers (reported in recent years to number around 42 members) that calculates and publishes the official "**Avurudu Nekath Seettuwa**" — auspicious times for lighting the hearth, starting work, eating the first meal, and other New Year rituals — disseminated nationally via television, radio, and newspapers. This process has, in some years (e.g., 2017 and 2024), become publicly contentious, with dissenting astrologers or rival groups publishing alternative times and disputing the official committee's calculations, illustrating both the seriousness attached to nekath timing and the lack of a single unchallenged astrological authority.
- **Professional association.** Sri Lanka has a statutory professional body for the field: the **Astrologers' Association of Sri Lanka**, incorporated by Act No. 34 of 1997. The Act establishes the Association as a body corporate empowered to conduct classes, lectures, and seminars on astrology and to hold examinations and award certificates to those who complete them — a self-regulating professional and educational body, though not a state licensing regime comparable to those governing medicine or law.

## "Hela Astrology" and Indigenous Framing

In some contemporary Sri Lankan discourse, the term **"Hela" astrology** or **"Hela Jothisha"** is used to frame the practice as a distinctly Sinhala/indigenous tradition — echoing a broader current of 20th-century Sinhala cultural nationalism associated with "Hela" (an older name for the Sinhala people and language). The best-documented expression of this framing is the linguistic movement **Hela Havula**, founded in 1941 by grammarian Munidasa Cumaratunga, which promoted a "purified" Sinhala identity emphasizing indigenous roots over Sanskrit, Pali, and colonial-era foreign influence.

By extension, some modern practitioners and writers use "Hela Jothisha" to emphasize the Sinhala/Buddhist character of the island's astrology and distinguish it rhetorically from Hindu-inflected Indian Vedic astrology, even though the underlying computational system (nakshatras, rashis, grahas, dashas) is technically shared with the Indian tradition. This is best understood as a distinction of **emphasis and framing** — indigenous naming, Buddhist interpretation, cultural ownership — rather than a wholly separate technical system. Mainstream professional astrology in Sri Lanka, including the government's official New Year nekath process and the Astrologers' Association, operates on the shared Vedic-derived framework described above, regardless of whether individual practitioners describe their work using "Hela" terminology.
