# Ethics of Practice in Sri Lankan Astrology (Hela Jothisha)

Astrology in Sri Lanka — known locally as Jothisha or Hela Jothisha — occupies a respected place in Sinhala Buddhist culture, guiding decisions around marriage, house-building, naming, career, and auspicious timing (nekath). Because astrologers are consulted at moments of real vulnerability — a troubled marriage, a family illness, a major financial decision — the practice carries genuine ethical weight. This document surveys ethical principles articulated by established international astrological bodies and considers how they apply, and sometimes strain, in the Sri Lankan context. It does not attempt to settle astrology's scientific status; it treats astrology as a widely practiced advisory tradition and asks what responsible, harm-avoiding practice looks like within it.

## The state of formal ethics codes in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka does have a legally recognized astrologers' body: the **Astrologers' Association of Sri Lanka**, incorporated by the **Astrologers' Association of Sri Lanka (Incorporation) Act, No. 34 of 1997**. The Act makes the Association a body corporate empowered to conduct classes, lectures, and examinations in astrology, award certificates to those who pass, and confer honours on eminent astrologers.

Based on available public sources, this incorporation functions as a professional and educational body rather than a binding regulatory authority. It does not appear to run a public disciplinary process, a mandatory license-to-practice regime, or an enforceable client-facing code of conduct comparable to medical or legal professional councils. Anyone in Sri Lanka can call themselves an astrologer and take paying clients without certification from the Association or any government body. This should be stated plainly: **Sri Lanka has professional astrology bodies and academic programs, but no general licensing regime enforcing client-protection standards on all practitioners.**

On the academic side, several universities teach astrology formally. The **University of Kelaniya's Department of Sanskrit and Eastern Studies** offers a Certificate Course in Astrology (in Sinhala, roughly six months), alongside related courses in Vasthu Vidya and Yoga Philosophy. The **Bhiksu University of Sri Lanka** offers an external Diploma in Astrology. These are genuine certificate- and diploma-level offerings rather than full honours degrees dedicated to astrology, but they show Sri Lankan academia treats jothisha as worthy of systematic study — strengthening the case that its practitioners should be held to comparable standards of rigor and care as other advisory professions.

In the absence of one enforceable Sri Lankan client-ethics code, this document draws on the published codes of the **American Federation of Astrologers (AFA)**, the **International Society for Astrological Research (ISAR)**, and the **National Council for Geocosmic Research (NCGR)**, adapting their principles to Sinhala astrological practice.

## Informed consent and honesty about limitations

ISAR's code frames the astrologer's prime directive as "do no harm" and calls for honesty that avoids "false hopes" as much as needless fear. NCGR's code requires members to present interpretations "with objectivity and appropriate qualifying statements, rather than as final or unequivocal pronouncements."

Applied to Jothisha, this means being transparent that a horoscope reading offers one interpretive lens on a situation, not a certain forecast. Predictions about marriage, career timing, or health should be framed as guidance for reflection, and clients should understand what a reading is and isn't claiming to know before they pay for it. This matters especially in Sri Lanka, where a nekath or porondama reading is often treated by families as close to a final verdict on a major decision, so the astrologer's framing of certainty carries outsized practical weight.

## Avoiding fear-based or coercive predictions

This is one of the most well-documented failure modes in astrology practice worldwide. Confident, specifically named predictions of catastrophe — death, serious illness, financial ruin, curses — are a recognized warning sign of unethical practice, because such predictions are often used to frighten clients into buying expensive "remedies" from the very astrologer who diagnosed the problem. ISAR's code commits members to "never needlessly frighten others with extreme and insensitive predictions," and NCGR's code prohibits statements causing "harm through confusion, misunderstanding or fear."

Locally, this maps onto predictions of "doshas" (afflictions) allegedly neutralizable only through specific poojas, gemstones, or rituals sold by the same astrologer. Ethical practice means not manufacturing urgency around such diagnoses, not claiming exclusive ability to "fix" a problem one has just identified, and not leveraging a client's grief or family conflict into a sale. Where remedial rituals reflect genuine Sinhala Buddhist or Hindu tradition, they should be presented as optional and meaningful, not as mandatory interventions to avert disaster.

## Financial ethics: fees and the remedy problem

Established codes require transparent, disclosed fees agreed before consultation, and prohibit exploiting clients' distress for gain. A recognized red flag arises when expensive remedies — gemstones, yantras, poojas — are available only from the astrologer who diagnosed the "problem," creating a direct conflict of interest between diagnosis and cure.

Ethical practice means separating diagnosis from remedy wherever possible, disclosing any markup or personal benefit from a recommended remedy, never presenting a remedy as mandatory or time-critical, and never pricing it to exploit fear or financial desperation. Clients facing family crises — illness, unemployment, troubled marriages — are especially vulnerable to this kind of upselling, raising the ethical bar for the astrologer to resist it.

## Confidentiality of birth and personal data

A reading typically requires a client's exact birth date, time, and place, alongside private discussion of health, marriage, finances, and family conflict. NCGR's code requires protecting client information and identity, sharing data only with consent. Applied locally: birth details and personal disclosures should stay confidential, not be shared with other family members without consent (a real risk when parents commission a match for an adult child), and not appear in promotional materials or "case studies" without explicit permission.

## Respecting client autonomy and free will

ISAR's code calls for astrologers to "recognize [clients'] autonomy and encourage them to make their own decisions" — a crucial corrective to treating astrology as deterministic fate that removes personal responsibility. A horoscope should be presented as one input among many for a client's own reflection, not an unchangeable verdict. A client should never be told a marriage or venture is doomed regardless of effort, in ways that discourage reasonable due diligence.

## Cultural sensitivity: porondam, caste, and family bias

Porondam (horoscope matching before marriage) is a deeply established practice, and evaluating compatibility is a legitimate part of the astrological tradition. The genuine ethical tension is how this practice can be, and in South Asian societies broadly has been, used as cover for family objections rooted in caste, class, or economic status rather than genuine astrological concern. A family opposed to a match for social reasons can invoke an unfavorable reading as a face-saving justification, and an astrologer who bends a reading to fit a paying family's preferences becomes complicit in reinforcing that bias.

An ethical astrologer applies consistent, disclosed methodology rather than tailoring a "match" verdict to what a client wants to hear, declines to let a reading be used as a pretext for discrimination, and is honest that horoscope compatibility is one traditional consideration among many for a marriage, not a determinative one. This is a matter of professional integrity rather than settled law, but it is a real, documented hazard in South Asian horoscope-matching practice.

## Professional competence and referral

Ethical codes hold that practitioners should work "within the boundaries of their competence" and refer clients elsewhere when a matter falls outside it. For Jothisha practitioners, the key application is this: never advise a client to forgo or delay medical treatment, psychological care, or legal counsel in favor of an astrological remedy. Where a client shows signs of illness, mental health distress, or a legal dispute, the responsible course is to encourage — not replace — qualified professional help, treating astrological or spiritual practice as a complement, never a substitute. Continuing education, through the Astrologers' Association of Sri Lanka, university courses, or peer study, is part of this same obligation to competence.

## Conclusion

Sri Lanka has real institutional infrastructure around astrology — an incorporated professional association, university certificate and diploma courses, and a long practice tradition — but no binding, enforced code of client-protection ethics comparable to licensed professions. In that gap, the principles developed by international astrological bodies — do no harm, honesty about limitations, confidentiality, respect for autonomy, financial transparency, and referral to appropriate professionals — offer a workable, culturally adaptable foundation, addressing the specific pressure points of Sri Lankan practice: fear-driven remedy sales, the confidentiality of deeply personal consultations, and the risk that horoscope matching becomes a vehicle for caste or class bias rather than a genuine tool for reflection.
